Assignment 0 - Exploring the History of Computing

Historical note

Today, we take computers for granted, but this was not always the case. In fact, the word computer originally referred to a person, not a machine. Before electronic computers existed, human computers performed calculations by hand for astronomy, navigation, engineering, and science.

Modern computing did not emerge from a single invention or a single inventor. It developed gradually as people found new ways to represent information, automate calculations, give instructions to machines, and make those machines more reliable and accessible.

Here are a few landmarks to help you see the larger story:

  • Human computers: teams of people carried out long and difficult calculations by hand.
  • Mechanical calculation: inventors designed machines that could automate arithmetic.
  • Programmable machines: Charles Babbage and Ada Lovelace explored the idea of a general-purpose machine controlled by instructions.
  • Foundations of modern computing: Alan Turing, Konrad Zuse, Grace Hopper, and many others developed fundamental ideas about computation, programming, hardware, and software.
  • Electronic and personal computers: computers became faster, smaller, and eventually available outside governments, universities, and large corporations.

Why this matters for CS1

Computer Science is not just about learning syntax or writing code that “works.” It is about learning how humans formalize ideas, build abstractions, make mistakes, revise their thinking, and gradually create reliable systems.

Many of the people you will encounter here worked with incomplete tools, wrong assumptions, and limited technology—yet their ideas shaped how we program today. As you begin learning Processing and Java-style thinking, you will use a similar cycle on a smaller scale: experiment, identify mistakes, revise, and improve.

Your assignment

Explore at least two of the resources below. You may also conduct independent research, but it is not required. Then write a 300–500 word reflection that develops one or two ideas rather than simply listing facts.

You may use the following questions to guide your reflection:

  • How did the meaning of the word computer change over time?
  • Which development represented the greatest conceptual change, rather than simply a technological improvement?
  • Is there a person in the history of computing whose work particularly interests you? What did this person contribute, and why does that contribution matter?
  • What part of this history challenges the familiar story of a lone inventor creating something entirely new?
  • What connection can you make between one historical development and something we will do in CS1?

Cite at least two sources. A link to each source is sufficient; you do not need to use a formal citation style. After posting your reflection, respond thoughtfully to at least one classmate. Your response should add an idea, question, or connection rather than simply say that you agree.

How this is graded

  • Level 3: a thoughtful, clearly written reflection that develops ideas, makes meaningful connections, cites at least two sources, and includes a substantive response to a classmate.
  • Level 2: a solid reflection that addresses the topic and cites sources but needs more explanation, clearer connections, or a more substantive response to a classmate.
  • Level 1: a brief or mostly factual response with limited reflection, unclear connections, missing citations, or no response to a classmate.
  • Level 0: no submission.

Suggested resources

Human computers

  • Radhanath Sikdar — a mathematician employed as a human computer who helped calculate the height of Mount Everest.
  • From Hidden to Modern Figures — NASA’s account of the women whose mathematical work supported the United States space program.

Early machines and programming

  • Charles Babbage — designs for mechanical calculating machines, including the Analytical Engine.
  • Ada Lovelace — an early description of how a general-purpose machine could follow an algorithm.
  • Konrad Zuse — programmable digital computers and an early high-level programming language.

Foundations of modern computing

  • Alan Turing — theoretical computation, codebreaking, and early artificial intelligence.
  • Grace Hopper — compilers, programming languages, and the effort to make programming more accessible.
  • An Illustrated History of Computers — a broader visual overview of computing history.

Personal computing

  • Altair 8800 — an influential early personal computer.

For those interested in learning more, I recommend Walter Isaacson’s The Innovators: How a Group of Hackers, Geniuses, and Geeks Created the Digital Revolution (Simon & Schuster). It begins with Ada Lovelace and explores the work of Vannevar Bush, Alan Turing, John von Neumann, and many others.
